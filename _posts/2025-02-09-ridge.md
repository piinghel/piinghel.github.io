Combining stock signals creates two different problems. First, we need to decide
which economic ideas deserve a place in the model. Then we need to decide how to
weight several measurements of those ideas when they overlap.

A simple multifactor score answers both questions by construction: select a few
interpretable factors, rank the stocks, and give every factor the same vote.
Ridge regression keeps the final score linear, but lets historical data determine
the weights across a much broader predictor set.

Fitting that regression is easy. Establishing what it has learned is harder. A
higher backtest Sharpe ratio could mean that Ridge orders stocks more accurately.
It could also mean that the score has rediscovered a low-volatility tilt, changed
the gross exposure produced by volatility scaling, or benefited from one lucky
rebalance calendar.

This article therefore asks more than whether the final performance number goes
up. Does Ridge improve the out-of-sample ordering of stocks? Are its fitted
relationships stable enough to interpret? Which characteristics does the
portfolio actually own? And does the result survive changes that should not
contain information, such as adding placebo predictors?

The comparison is deliberately controlled. Both scores use the same point-in-time
Russell 1000 universe and feed the same 75-long/75-short selection, volatility
scaling, execution lag, transaction costs, and rebalance calendars. The ranking
rule is the only downstream input that changes. Ridge does see many more inputs,
so this is a comparison of two complete specifications—not learned versus equal
weights on an identical feature set.

## The multifactor model

The benchmark gives equal weight to five economic themes. Each theme is converted
to a daily cross-sectional rank and signed so that a higher score is more
attractive:

- **Defensive risk:** low realized volatility over 21, 63, and 126 sessions,
  together with a low third-largest daily gain over the past month.
- **Momentum:** high returns over 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, with the most recent
  month skipped.
- **Short interest:** low publication-lagged shares short relative to average
  trading volume.
- **Size:** high market capitalization—a large-cap stability tilt, not a
  small-cap premium.
- **Return consistency:** a low fraction of negative days over the previous
  three years.

The two defensive components are averaged first. Defensive risk therefore has
the same 20% weight as momentum, short interest, size, and return consistency;
it does not receive extra influence merely because it has more measurements. A
stock needs all five theme scores, so missing values never cause the remaining
themes to be silently reweighted.

This group-balanced construction is the benchmark. Its strength is attribution:
every stock score can be traced to five signed ranks with fixed weights. Its cost
is rigidity. It assumes that the five themes are equally useful at every point
in the sample and cannot distinguish between horizons within a theme.

## Ridge regression

Ridge regression starts from 191 predictors rather than five finished theme
scores. The large number is less exotic than it sounds: most columns are the
same economic variable measured over different horizons or transformed in a
different way.

- **Return history and consistency (32):** recent returns, momentum at several
  horizons, historical Sharpe ratios, volatility-scaled returns, and loss
  frequency.
- **Risk and price state (55):** realized and downside volatility, average true
  range, RSI, MACD, Bollinger position, moving-average distance, location within
  recent price ranges, and trend persistence. The last measure is the share of
  the past 252 sessions that the stock closed above its 200-session moving
  average.
- **Market capitalization (36):** the current level, changes, rolling moments,
  and distance from previous highs and lows.
- **Trading activity and short interest (64):** volume levels and changes,
  volume relative to its own history, and publication-lagged shorting pressure.
- **Market relationship (4):** rolling correlations with the Russell 1000 over
  126, 252, 504, and 756 sessions. I exclude the 21- and 63-session versions
  because this predictor is intended to describe structural market integration,
  not short-lived co-movement.

These labels are for explanation; they do not constrain the regression. A
20-session return and a 252-session return receive separate coefficients even
though both belong to return history. The four market-correlation features
describe a stock. They are distinct from the realized market beta of the final
long-short portfolio.

## Target and normalization

One observation is a stock on a signal date. The raw target is the stock's
risk-adjusted return over the next 20 sessions:

$$
q_{i,t}^{20}
=
\sqrt{252}\,
\frac{\operatorname{mean}(r_{i,t+1:t+20})}
     {\operatorname{stdev}(r_{i,t+1:t+20})}.
$$

This is a forward Sharpe-like quantity, not the next 20-session return. A stock
can therefore rank well either by earning more or by reaching a given return
with a smoother path. That choice matters later: a model trained on a
risk-adjusted target has a direct reason to prefer lower-risk stocks.

I convert the target to a cross-sectional percentile rank within each BICS
sector on each date and map it to $[-1,1]$. Ridge is thus trained to answer a
relative question: which stocks are likely to deliver better risk-adjusted
returns than their sector peers over the next month? The portfolio itself is not
explicitly sector neutral, so sector-relative training reduces one source of
variation without guaranteeing neutral sector holdings.

Predictors are normalized separately. NaNs and infinities are converted to
missing values, then each stock's predictor history is forward-filled. Any
value still missing is replaced by the same-date sector mean. Each predictor is
then ranked across the full stock universe on that date and mapped to $[-1,1]$:

$$
x_{i,t}^{p,\mathrm{rank}}
=
2\,\operatorname{PctRank}_{t}(X_{i,t}^{p})-1.
$$

The common scale is essential because the Ridge penalty acts on coefficient
magnitude. Ranking also limits the influence of raw outliers and avoids using
future distributional parameters. Its cost is loss of distance information:
the model sees the ordering of market capitalizations or volatilities, not how
far apart their raw values are. The unrestricted forward fill is another
limitation: it prevents avoidable gaps but does not impose a maximum age on a
stale observation.

## What Ridge regression learns

The model is linear:

$$
\hat y_i = \hat\beta_0 + \mathbf{x}_i^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}.
$$

I estimate the coefficients with scikit-learn's Ridge regression. Its fitted
objective is squared prediction error plus an $L_2$ penalty:

$$
\underset{\beta_0,\boldsymbol\beta}{\min}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\left(y_i-\beta_0-\mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\right)^2
+ \alpha\sum_{j=1}^{191}\beta_j^2,
\qquad \alpha=1.
$$

The penalty pulls coefficients toward zero without setting them exactly to zero.
That is appropriate for nearby momentum windows, volatility estimates, and
volume statistics that describe much of the same variation. But it does not
make individual coefficients uniquely identifiable. When two predictors are
strongly correlated, Ridge can distribute their combined effect across both;
coefficient size is evidence of model reliance, not a causal importance score.

The scaling of this objective is easy to overlook. Scikit-learn does not divide
the squared-error term by the number of observations, so the effective strength
of $\alpha=1$ depends on the sample size. With millions of stock-date rows, the
penalty is modest in well-identified directions and matters most where
correlation makes the design matrix nearly singular. I use the fixed value as a
pre-specified production choice; I do not assume it is strong enough to dismiss
irrelevant predictors without a separate placebo test.

The predictors share a scale, so coefficient paths are numerically comparable.
I still interpret them at three levels: whether a coefficient keeps its sign
across walk-forward fits, whether an economic family remains important when
individual columns exchange weight, and whether the fitted score becomes an
actual portfolio tilt after stock selection and volatility scaling. Those are
different questions and need different diagnostics.

For each training window, I fit three Ridge regressions. One uses every third
date starting with the first date, the others start with the second and third.
I average their predictions so that the score depends less on one particular
subsample of training dates. This is an implementation detail of the Ridge
regression used here, not a separate model family.

## Keeping future information out

The model is evaluated with an expanding walk-forward procedure. The first
training window spans 900 market dates. I leave a 21-date gap after the training
sample, predict the next 600 dates, add that test block to the history, and fit
again. Training always starts at the beginning of the sample.

The gap is not cosmetic. A target dated $t$ uses returns through $t+20$. Leaving
21 dates between the final training observation and the test block prevents
prices used to construct a training target from crossing into the test period.

Joining the non-overlapping test blocks gives one out-of-sample prediction

five-factor research itself was chosen on a separate untouched holdout.

## From rankings to portfolios

The data run from 3 January 1995 through 27 May 2026 and cover roughly 3,400
stocks over time. A stock must be a Russell 1000 constituent on the relevant
date. To trade, it must also have an unadjusted price of at least $5, pass the
announced-merger screen, and survive issuer share-class deduplication.

On every rebalance date, I buy the 75 highest-scoring stocks and short the 75
lowest. Positions begin with equal signal weights and are then scaled by each
stock's trailing 60-session volatility. Estimated volatility has a 5% floor,
individual weights are capped at 4%, and each sleeve has a 100% gross limit.

I evaluate three Friday calendars. Each rebalances every three weeks, with a
different starting week. Signals trade at the next close, and every dollar
traded incurs 5 bp of cost. The reported metrics are the mean of the three
calendar-level results; I do not first blend their daily returns into an
artificially smoother portfolio.

Ridge regression improves the ordering of stocks before portfolio construction.
Its mean daily cross-sectional Spearman correlation with the realized target is
0.0439, compared with 0.0206 for the multifactor score. Because adjacent
20-session outcomes overlap, a naive t-statistic would overstate the evidence.
Using a 20-lag Newey–West adjustment gives t-statistics of 12.57 for Ridge
regression and 2.39 for the multifactor score.

The portfolio result is more restrained. Net return rises by only 0.04
percentage points. The 0.26 improvement in Sharpe ratio comes mainly from lower
realized volatility, and the worst drawdown is 13.50 percentage points smaller.
Market beta is nearly unchanged, so the smoother return path is not explained by
a large reduction in broad equity exposure.

There is a cost. Average long-plus-short turnover per rebalance is 1.70× equity
for Ridge regression and 0.78× for the multifactor model. The two strategies'
gross daily returns have a correlation of 0.61, which also shows that Ridge
regression is not merely reproducing the same ranking with slightly different
weights.

Neither score works in every period. From 2 January through 27 May 2026, the
multifactor model has a net Sharpe ratio of −0.66 and Ridge regression has a net
Sharpe of 0.03. The full-period advantage is evidence of a better historical
risk profile, not a guarantee that the model adapts successfully to every
regime.

## What the comparison tells me

The results favor Ridge regression on risk-adjusted performance, but they do not
amount to a clean model victory. Ridge turns a broad, correlated feature set
into a ranking that realizes less volatility and a smaller drawdown while
preserving roughly the same return.

That improvement comes with two qualifications. First, Ridge regression sees
191 predictors while the multifactor score sees five group-balanced factors.
This is not a clean test of learned versus equal weights on identical inputs.
Second, the predictions are walk-forward out of sample, but the earlier factor
research used the available history. There is no untouched factor-selection
holdout.

The practical choice is therefore narrower than “machine learning wins.” The
multifactor score is easier to inspect, cheaper to trade, and earns almost the
same return. Ridge regression earns its place only if the smoother historical
path is worth the additional estimation and turnover.
