A stock-selection model rarely begins with one clean predictor. It begins with
many related measures of momentum, risk, liquidity, size, and trading activity,
often calculated over several overlapping horizons. The practical question is
how to combine them into one ranking without letting small differences in the
sample produce unstable weights.

A 21-session volatility rank and a 63-session downside-volatility rank, for
example, are different measurements of much the same defensive idea. Small
sample differences should not decide which one dominates the final ranking.

I use [Ridge regression](https://doi.org/10.1080/00401706.1970.10488634) to turn
these related measures into one score and compare it with a simpler fixed-weight
multifactor score. Both approaches begin with the same Russell 1000 membership
and trading rules, then use the same portfolio construction, rebalance dates,
execution timing, and trading costs. They differ in the predictors they receive
and in how those predictors are combined. Predictor availability also leaves
their final tradable cross-sections slightly different, a limitation I return
to below.

## Research question

The central question is: **how can we combine many overlapping predictors into
one reliable out-of-sample stock ranking?**

Here, reliability has three observable parts: the average daily IC, its
day-to-day variability, and the net return and risk that remain after portfolio
construction.

The short answer is that the Ridge score has steadier out-of-sample ranking
quality than the fixed-weight benchmark, with only a modest difference in
average signal strength. Mean daily IC rises from 0.0420 to 0.0438, while daily
IC volatility falls from 0.1215 to 0.0871. Full-period net return remains close
to the fixed-weight benchmark, while portfolio volatility falls by about 26%
and maximum drawdown improves by roughly 13 percentage points. The cost of that
difference is turnover: Ridge trades a little more than twice as much.

I examine that question through four views. The information coefficient measures
the quality and stability of the ranking before portfolio construction. Net
performance shows what survives stock selection, volatility scaling, and costs.
The fitted coefficients show how Ridge combines the inputs through time, while
the final portfolio exposures show which characteristics actually reach the
long and short books. These are diagnostics for the same signal-construction
problem.

## A fixed mix and a learned mix

The research panel uses Russell 1000 membership for the current calendar month
on each date. Model training begins with that membership cross-section. The
portfolio then removes stocks below $5, announced merger targets, and duplicate
share classes from the same issuer. These additional screens keep the traded
book focused on reasonably tradable common stocks and avoid giving special-event
returns or multiple listings of one company disproportionate influence. Across
the Ridge out-of-sample dates, 841 to 1,023 stocks have the required inputs and
pass the trading screens, with a median of 973.

Price-based predictors and the forward target use adjusted closing prices; in
the target formula below, $r$ is the one-session percentage return. Short
interest is delayed by 21 sessions before it enters a predictor, reflecting its
publication lag.

The benchmark combines five signed themes: defensive risk, momentum, short
interest, size, and return consistency. After averaging the two defensive
sub-signals, each theme receives the same weight. For stock $i$ on date $t$,

$$
s^{\mathrm{MF}}_{i,t}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=1}^{5}z_{i,t,k},
$$

where $z_{i,t,k}$ is the cross-sectional percentile rank of theme $k$, oriented
so that a higher value is always more attractive. Its absolute scale does not
matter for selection; its ordering does. Once chosen, the signs and weights
remain fixed throughout the portfolio test. Higher scores favor lower volatility
and fewer large daily jumps,
stronger 3- to 12-month momentum excluding the latest month, lower short
interest relative to volume, larger market capitalization, and fewer negative
return sessions.

This $1/5$ benchmark is transparent, but it is not uniquely correct. The choice
of five themes, the defensive sub-signal average, the lookback horizons, and the
equal weights are all subjective design decisions. It is therefore a useful
reference point rather than a neutral definition of a multifactor model. A
different fixed-weight benchmark could make the comparison look better or
worse, which is another reason to treat the current result as evidence about
these two implementations rather than a final verdict on Ridge.

Ridge uses 144 individual predictors. The library covers trend and reversal,
risk, liquidity and trading activity, size, short interest, and co-movement
with the Russell 1000. Most ideas are measured over several horizons, with each
horizon entering separately.

The deck began with 191 candidates. I removed invalid or persistently redundant
variants, then used group-level sensitivity tests to decide several
volume-related removals and additions. Those decisions used this historical
sample. The walk-forward predictions are therefore out of sample conditional
on a predictor library that was itself developed in sample. The families below
are only a map for the reader: Ridge receives every column separately and does
not assign weights at the family level.

There is an important asymmetry here. The benchmark combines five predefined
themes, while Ridge receives 144 individual predictor columns. The test compares
two complete ways to build a stock ranking; it does not isolate the choice of
estimator from the effect of using the broader predictor library.

The required inputs also produce different coverage. The fixed-weight benchmark
has a median of 915 tradable scores per date, compared with 973 for Ridge. On a
common date, about 14 of the 150 names selected by Ridge are unavailable to the
benchmark on average. Table 1 avoids this problem by evaluating IC only on
common stock-dates, but the portfolio comparison lets each method trade its own
available cross-section. Performance can therefore reflect coverage as well as
the score itself. A matched-universe portfolio test is needed to separate those
effects.

## Target and cross-sectional setup

The prediction target is the stock's Sharpe ratio over the following 20 market
sessions,

$$
\mathrm{SR}^{(20)}_{i,t}
=\sqrt{252}\,
\frac{\overline r_{i,t+1:t+20}}
{\sigma\!\left(r_{i,t+1:t+20}\right)}.
$$

I use forward Sharpe instead of forward return because the desired ranking
should reward a return that is persistent over the month more than an equally
large return produced by a few volatile days. A raw-return target would give
those volatile moves more influence and could make the model learn a high-risk
tilt. This is still a ranking target—it does not directly optimize the eventual
portfolio Sharpe—and the annualization factor does not change the ordering.

This creates a deliberate asymmetry between target and predictor normalization.
The target asks for an order within current industry peers, while full-universe
predictor ranks can still express broad cross-sector differences. Ranking the
predictors within sector as well would remove that information before the model
saw it. Sector-and-date ranking also removes the level and magnitude of
between-sector differences from the target: the model sees who ranks higher
inside each sector, not whether one sector had a higher raw forward Sharpe than
another. Date-only target ranks preserve cross-sector ordering, though not the
original magnitudes. The choice is intentional, but it should be tested: the
alternatives could change both the IC comparison and the portfolio's sector
exposures.

On each date, I rank the target within each Bloomberg Industry Classification
System (BICS) Level 1 sector and map the rank to $[-1,1]$. Ridge therefore
learns a stock's position relative to contemporaneous sector peers, not the raw
difference in forward Sharpe. This reduces the influence of sector-level
outcome differences, but it also discards their magnitude and gives sectors
with more stocks more training observations. It does not make the final
portfolio sector neutral: selection is still global and no sector constraint
is imposed on the book.

Predictors are ranked across the current-membership training cross-section on
the same date and mapped to $[-1,1]$. Ranking makes unlike inputs comparable and
limits the influence of extreme raw observations. Missing observations are
carried forward within a stock where possible, then filled with that date's
sector average before ranking. The price, merger-target, and share-class screens
are applied to portfolio eligibility rather than to this normalization
cross-section. The model can therefore learn from a member that is not eligible
to trade on that date, while the final portfolio cannot select it.

The main comparison through Figure 6 uses this sector-ranked forward-Sharpe
target. It is a starting specification, not evidence that Sharpe is inherently
better than return. I test that choice after the coefficient and exposure
results.

## Ridge estimation

With that ranked target and predictor matrix, each walk-forward fit solves

$$
(\widehat\beta_{0,t},\widehat\beta_t)
=\arg\min_{\beta_0,\beta}
\sum_{(i,s)\in\mathcal T_t}
\left(y^{(20)}_{i,s}-\beta_0-x_{i,s}^{\top}\beta\right)^2
+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2,
\qquad \lambda=1.
$$

Here $\beta_0$ is the intercept, $\beta$ is the vector of predictor
coefficients, and $\mathcal T_t$ contains the stock-date observations available
to fit the model at time $t$.

The penalty discourages large individual coefficients. When several inputs
describe a similar effect over nearby horizons, Ridge tends to distribute
weight across them, making one window less likely to dominate because of a
small sample difference. I hold $\lambda=1$ fixed across all fits rather than
retuning it in each window. Because the training sample expands while the
penalty remains fixed, its effective strength relative to the data gradually
declines.

## Walk-forward estimation

The first fit uses 900 market dates. I then leave a 21-date gap before scoring
the next 600 dates. The gap keeps the forward 20-session target in the training
sample from overlapping the first test observation. After each test block, the
training history expands and Ridge is fitted again. Within each window, I split
the ordered training dates into three interleaved samples: the first model sees
dates 1, 4, 7, and so on; the second sees dates 2, 5, 8; and the third sees
dates 3, 6, 9. I fit one model to each sample and average their predictions.
This thins the heavily overlapping adjacent targets without making the result
depend on one arbitrary starting phase. The 900-date starting window gives the
first fit several years of cross-sections while preserving a long out-of-sample
record. The 600-date test blocks let us observe each fitted score for roughly
two and a half years before it is updated.

## Portfolio construction and trading costs

On every rebalance date, each method selects the 75 highest-ranked stocks for
the long side and the 75 lowest-ranked stocks for the short side. Seventy-five
names retain breadth without turning the portfolio into a near-universe
holding. I use the same stock-level volatility-scaling rule developed in my
[low-volatility portfolio-construction
article](/quant/2024/12/15/low-volatility-factor.html#the-main-implementation-stock-level-volatility-scaling)
and hold it fixed for both rankings. The ranking chooses the stocks; a separate
60-session volatility estimate determines their relative sizes. Each stock
starts at $1/75$, is scaled by 20% divided by its trailing 60-session
volatility, and is capped at 4% of sleeve capital. Estimated volatility is
floored at 5%, the scaling multiplier is capped at four, and each side is
scaled down if its gross exposure exceeds 100%; a side below 100% is not scaled
up.

The portfolio has three equal-capital sleeves. Each sleeve rebalances every
third Friday, with the sleeves offset by one week, so one-third of the capital
is refreshed each week. Each sleeve is held for three weeks, somewhat shorter
than the 20-session prediction horizon; I use the shorter schedule to refresh
the ranking more often, while staggering the sleeves reduces dependence on one
particular Friday. Orders execute at the following close, one trading day after
the signal. This delay prevents the backtest from using a closing price that
was not yet available when the ranking was formed.

Reported net returns charge 5 basis points for every dollar traded, including
the initial portfolio formation. Two-way turnover is the sum of absolute long-
and short-side trading divided by equity. Borrow fees, financing, market
impact, and taxes are not included. I return to the realized turnover and cost
drag alongside the performance results.

## Ranking quality

The daily information coefficient is the cross-sectional Spearman correlation
between the score and the subsequently realized target:

$$
\mathrm{IC}_t=\rho_{\mathrm S}\!\left(\widehat s_{i,t},y^{(20)}_{i,t}\right).
$$

It measures whether higher-scored stocks subsequently rank above lower-scored
stocks. Figure 2 plots the cumulative sum, $C_T=\sum_{t\le T}\mathrm{IC}_t$.
The path summarizes ranking quality through time: a steeper slope means a
stronger average daily ordering, while flat or falling stretches mark weak or
negative rank correlation. I calculate it on every common out-of-sample
prediction date, not only on portfolio rebalance dates, and use the same
sector-ranked 20-session target for both methods. Consecutive IC observations
share much of the same forward window, so they are serially dependent. I use
mean IC divided by daily IC volatility as a descriptive, unannualized stability
measure, not as a significance statistic.

## How Ridge combines the predictors

The heatmap keeps the ten predictors with the largest mean absolute coefficient
and shows their stored block-level value in all 12 expanding walk-forward
blocks. Each cell is the signed coefficient averaged across the three
interleaved ensemble members; blue cells are positive and muted red cells are
negative. Because every predictor is cross-sectionally rank-normalized, the
coefficients are comparable within this model. The year under a column is the
start of that block's out-of-sample test period. On narrow screens, the heatmap
keeps six representative blocks, including the first and last.

Read each coefficient as the predictor's contribution after the other inputs
have been accounted for. A positive value links a higher predictor rank to a
higher forecast of sector-relative forward Sharpe; a negative value reverses
that relation. It is a conditional association, not a causal effect or a clean
stand-alone measure of importance. Correlated predictors can share the same
underlying signal, so the group of related weights is often more informative
than one cell on its own.

## What reaches the portfolio

Model coefficients and portfolio tilts answer different questions. A
coefficient describes how an input contributes to the score while holding the
other inputs in the model. The portfolio tilt also reflects cross-sectional
correlation, stock selection, and volatility scaling.

Each stock's predictor value is a cross-sectional rank between $-1$ and $1$.
To compute the book's tilt toward predictor $j$, I multiply that rank by the
stock's signed portfolio weight—positive for a long and negative for a short—
and divide the sum by the gross weight covered by that predictor:

$$
T_{j,t}=\frac{\sum_i w_{i,t}x_{i,j,t}}
{\sum_i\lvert w_{i,t}\rvert}.
$$

This keeps every tilt on the same intuitive scale. A positive value means the
long book owns higher ranks, the short book owns lower ranks, or both. A
negative value means the opposite; zero means no directional tilt. Figure 6
shows the ten predictors with the largest average absolute tilt. I calculate
the tilts from the held positions on every trading date, not only on rebalance
dates. All ten displayed predictors have complete coverage, so the denominator
is the portfolio's total gross weight on every date.

## Does the target choice matter?

The main specification predicts forward Sharpe because I want the ranking to
reward a return earned consistently across the next month. That is a modeling
choice, not a truth about the data. I therefore reran the same predictor
library, walk-forward dates, portfolio rules, and 5 bp cost assumption while
changing only the 20-session target or its cross-sectional normalization.

The normalization changes what the model is asked to learn. Sector ranking
asks it to order stocks relative to current sector peers. Date-only ranking
preserves cross-sector ordering, while raw targets also retain magnitude and
outliers. Figure 7 reports IC information ratios against each specification's
own target and the net Sharpe of the resulting portfolio. Because the IC target
changes across rows, portfolio Sharpe is the cleaner cross-target comparison.
The sensitivity runs also use each model's full eligible test sample, whereas
Table 1 restricts Ridge and the fixed-weight score to common stock-dates; this
is why the selected specification's IC information ratio is 0.54 here and 0.50
in Table 1.

The cleanest target comparison is between the two date-ranked versions because
both are mapped to the same $[-1,1]$ scale. Forward Sharpe reaches an IC
information ratio of 0.39 and a net portfolio Sharpe of 0.90, compared with
0.17 and 0.64 for forward return. In this implementation, rewarding the path
of returns produces a higher IC relative to its variability and a better
risk-adjusted portfolio than rewarding the 20-session move alone.

Sector ranking changes a different part of the result. For the Sharpe target,
mean IC falls from 5.07% to 4.56%, but daily IC volatility falls from 12.87% to
8.51%, lifting the IC information ratio from 0.39 to 0.54. Net return remains
near 7.1%, while annualized volatility declines from 7.96% to 7.40% and maximum
drawdown improves from 19.87% to 18.77%. Here, sector ranking helps mainly
through IC stability and portfolio risk rather than a higher average return.

I treat the raw-target results as stress tests, not clean evidence that one
target is intrinsically superior. Keeping $\lambda=1$ while changing the
target's units changes the effective strength of the Ridge penalty. A fairer
follow-up would standardize the target within each training window or tune the
penalty separately, then evaluate every score against one common realized
outcome.

## Limits of the test

The model predictions are genuinely walk-forward, but the broader research
process is not an untouched holdout: the predictor definitions and portfolio
choices were developed with knowledge of this historical sample. The results
should therefore be read as development evidence that needs confirmation on
new data.

The cost model is also deliberately simple. It charges a constant 5 basis
points per dollar traded, while real costs vary with liquidity, urgency, and
market conditions; borrow fees and financing are absent. Finally, ranking the
target within sectors focuses the learning problem on stock selection, but the
unconstrained portfolio can still acquire sector exposures. Those exposures
are a natural next diagnostic alongside the predictor tilts shown here.

The first follow-up should force both methods onto the same tradable stock-dates,
then hold the 144-column library fixed and compare equal weights, ordinary least
squares, and Ridge. That matched-universe, matched-feature test would separate
coverage, the choice of estimator, and the benefit of supplying the model with
more inputs. The predictor library should then be frozen and evaluated on newly
arriving data; uncertainty in the overlapping IC series should be estimated in
blocks. The comparison should also be repeated across holding periods, sector
constraints, and higher cost assumptions.

## What the combined score changes

Ridge gives us a practical estimator for aggregating the 144 related predictors
into one score. In this comparison, its resulting ranking has a modestly higher
average IC, a larger improvement in IC stability, and a portfolio with lower
volatility and drawdown. The clearest difference is steadier ranking quality and
a smoother realized portfolio. Net return remains similar over the full period
and is slightly lower in the recent windows. The trade-off is implementation:
Ridge turns the portfolio over about twice as much as the fixed-weight score
and absorbs a meaningfully larger cost charge. The lower realized risk remains visible after
applying the stated transaction-cost assumption.

The coefficient and exposure views add context. Ridge distributes weight across
several related trend, risk, liquidity, and size measures, while the constructed
portfolio ends up with concentrated low-risk and trend tilts.
Ridge is useful here as an estimator for combining many related predictors: it
lets the data assign weights while smoothing the coefficients of correlated
inputs, so one highly similar predictor is less likely to dominate because of
sample noise. This experiment supports further testing of the complete
144-predictor Ridge approach; it does not claim that one component alone caused
the improvement. The decisive next experiment is the matched
144-feature comparison, which would show how the estimator choice behaves when
the predictor library is held fixed.
