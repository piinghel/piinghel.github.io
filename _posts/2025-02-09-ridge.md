A stock-selection model rarely begins with one clean predictor. It begins with
many related measures of momentum, risk, liquidity, size, and trading activity,
often calculated over several overlapping horizons. The practical question is
how to combine them into one ranking without letting small differences in the
sample produce unstable weights.

I use Ridge regression for that combination and compare it with a simpler
fixed-weight multifactor score. Both approaches see the same point-in-time
Russell 1000 universe and use the same portfolio construction, rebalance dates,
execution timing, and trading costs. The score is the only part that changes.

## Research question

The central question is: **can Ridge combine many overlapping predictors into a
more reliable out-of-sample stock ranking than a fixed-weight multifactor
score?**

I answer it in stages. The information coefficient measures the quality and
stability of the ranking before portfolio construction. Net performance shows
what survives stock selection, volatility scaling, and costs. The fitted
coefficients show how Ridge combines the inputs through time, while the final
portfolio exposures show which characteristics actually reach the long and
short books. These are diagnostics for the same combination problem.

## A fixed mix and a learned mix

The investable universe is the point-in-time Russell 1000 on each date. I
remove stocks below $5, announced merger targets, and duplicate share classes
from the same issuer. These screens keep the comparison focused on reasonably
tradable common stocks and avoid giving special-event returns or multiple
listings of one company disproportionate influence.

The benchmark combines five signed themes: defensive risk, momentum, short
interest, size, and return consistency. After averaging the two defensive
sub-signals, each theme receives the same weight. For stock $i$ on date $t$,

$$
s^{\mathrm{MF}}_{i,t}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=1}^{5}z_{i,t,k},
$$

where $z_{i,t,k}$ is the cross-sectional rank of theme $k$, mapped to
$[-1,1]$. This is a transparent combination: the signs and weights are fixed
before the backtest.

Ridge uses 144 individual predictors. The library covers trend and reversal,
risk, liquidity and trading activity, size, short interest, and co-movement
with the Russell 1000. Most ideas are measured over several horizons, with each
horizon entering separately. At walk-forward fit $t$, Ridge solves

$$
(\widehat\beta_{0,t},\widehat\beta_t)
=\arg\min_{\beta_0,\beta}
\sum_{(i,s)\in\mathcal T_t}
\left(y^{(20)}_{i,s}-\beta_0-x_{i,s}^{\top}\beta\right)^2
+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2,
\qquad \lambda=1.
$$

The penalty shrinks related coefficients together. That matters here because
many inputs describe similar effects over nearby horizons. The model can share
weight across them instead of making one window dominate because of a small
sample difference. I keep $\lambda=1$ for every fit. With all inputs on the
same rank scale, this supplies a consistent amount of shrinkage without tuning
the penalty on later test results.

The 144 columns come from the predictor library below. I removed direct or
near-duplicate variants while retaining horizons that represent meaningfully
different holding periods. The families are only a map for the reader: Ridge
receives every column separately and does not assign weights at the family

## Target and cross-sectional setup

The prediction target is the stock's Sharpe ratio over the following 20 market
sessions,

$$
\mathrm{SR}^{(20)}_{i,t}
=\sqrt{252}\,
\frac{\overline r_{i,t+1:t+20}}
{\sigma\!\left(r_{i,t+1:t+20}\right)}.
$$

I use forward Sharpe instead of forward return because the desired ranking
should reward a return that is persistent over the month more than an equally
large return produced by a few volatile days. A raw-return target would give
those volatile moves more influence and could make the model learn a high-risk
tilt. This is still a ranking target—it does not directly optimize the eventual
portfolio Sharpe—and the annualization factor does not change the ordering.

I rank the target separately within each Bloomberg industry sector and date,
then map it to $[-1,1]$. This removes the market-wide level on a given date and
long-run return differences between sectors. The model therefore learns which
stocks outperform their contemporaneous sector peers. It does not make the
final portfolio sector neutral: selection is still global and no sector
constraint is imposed on the book.

Predictors are ranked across the full eligible universe on the same date and
also mapped to $[-1,1]$. Ranking makes unlike inputs comparable and limits the
influence of extreme raw observations. Missing observations are carried
forward within a stock where possible, then filled with that date's sector
average before ranking.

## Walk-forward estimation

The first fit uses 900 market dates. I then leave a 21-date gap before scoring
the next 600 dates. The gap keeps the forward 20-session target in the training
sample from overlapping the first test observation. After each test block, the
training history expands and Ridge is fitted again. Within each window, I split
the training dates into three interleaved samples, fit one model to each, and
average their predictions. The 900-date starting window gives the first fit
several years of cross-sections while preserving a long out-of-sample record.
The 600-date test blocks make the combination prove useful for roughly two and
a half years before it is updated; the interleaved fits reduce dependence on
one particular sequence of training dates.

## Portfolio construction and trading costs

On every rebalance date, each method selects the 75 highest-ranked stocks for
the long side and the 75 lowest-ranked stocks for the short side. Stocks begin
with equal signal weights and are then scaled by their trailing 60-session
volatility. Estimated volatility is floored at 5% annualized, leverage is
capped at four times, no stock can exceed 4% of a side, and each side is capped
at 100% gross exposure. Seventy-five names retain breadth without turning the
portfolio into a near-universe holding, while the 60-session estimate tempers
single-stock risk with a recent but less noisy window. My [low-volatility
article](/quant/2024/12/15/low-volatility-factor.html#the-main-implementation-stock-level-volatility-scaling)
develops this weighting rule in more detail.

The portfolio has three equal-capital sleeves. Each sleeve rebalances every
third Friday, with the sleeves offset by one week, so one-third of the capital
is refreshed each week. The three-week holding period is close to the
20-session prediction horizon, and staggering the sleeves reduces dependence
on one particular Friday. Orders execute at the following close, one trading
day after the signal. This delay prevents the backtest from using a closing
price that was not yet available when the ranking was formed.

Reported net returns charge 5 basis points for every dollar traded, including
the initial portfolio formation. Average two-way turnover per three-week sleeve
rebalance is 77.8% of equity for the fixed-weight score and 165.4% for Ridge,
equivalent to about 13.5 and 28.7 times equity per year. Ridge therefore pays
roughly twice as much in trading costs; the performance comparison below is
already after those costs.

## Ranking quality

The daily information coefficient is the cross-sectional Spearman correlation
between the score and the subsequently realized target:

$$
\mathrm{IC}_t=\rho_{\mathrm S}\!\left(\widehat s_{i,t},y^{(20)}_{i,t}\right).
$$

It measures whether higher-scored stocks subsequently rank above lower-scored
stocks. Figure 2 plots the cumulative sum, $C_T=\sum_{t\le T}\mathrm{IC}_t$.
The path summarizes ranking quality through time: a steeper slope means a
stronger average daily ordering, while flat or falling stretches mark weak or
negative rank correlation.

## How Ridge combines the predictors

The heatmap keeps the ten predictors with the largest mean absolute coefficient
and shows their value in all 12 expanding fits. Each number is the signed
coefficient from that fit; blue cells are positive and muted red cells are
negative. Because every predictor is cross-sectionally rank-normalized, the
coefficients are comparable within this model.

Read each coefficient as the predictor's contribution after the other inputs
have been accounted for. Correlated predictors can share the same underlying
signal, so the group of related weights is often more informative than one cell
on its own.

## What reaches the portfolio

Model coefficients and portfolio tilts answer different questions. A
coefficient describes how an input contributes to the score while holding the
other inputs in the model. The portfolio tilt also reflects cross-sectional
correlation, stock selection, and volatility scaling.

Each stock's predictor value is a cross-sectional rank between $-1$ and $1$.
To compute the book's tilt toward predictor $j$, I multiply that rank by the
stock's signed portfolio weight—positive for a long and negative for a short—
and divide the sum by the gross weight covered by that predictor:

$$
T_{j,t}=\frac{\sum_i w_{i,t}x_{i,j,t}}
{\sum_i\lvert w_{i,t}\rvert}.
$$

This keeps every tilt on the same intuitive scale. Zero means no directional
tilt, a positive value means the book leans toward higher predictor ranks, and
a negative value means it leans toward lower ranks. Figure 5 shows the ten
predictors with the largest average absolute tilt.

## What the combination changes

Ridge gives the 144 predictors a disciplined way to share weight. In this test,
that produces a modest increase in average IC, a larger improvement in IC
stability, and a portfolio with lower volatility and drawdown. The clearest
benefit is the steadier ranking and smoother portfolio. Raw return remains
similar over the full period and is slightly lower in the recent windows. The
trade-off is implementation: Ridge turns the portfolio over about twice as much
as the fixed-weight score and absorbs a meaningfully larger cost charge. The
reported risk improvement survives that charge.

The coefficient and exposure views complete that result. Ridge spreads weight
across several related trend, risk, liquidity, and size measures, while the
constructed portfolio ends up with concentrated low-risk and trend tilts. For
combining overlapping predictors, the useful feature of Ridge is the
regularization itself: it lets the data adjust the mix while keeping correlated
inputs from producing an erratic score.
